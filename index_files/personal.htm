GÜRDAL ARSLAN

UNIVERSITY OF HAWAI’I AT MĀNOA

Friday, February 19, 2010

 

Recently, I have some desire to write about my hiking experience in Hawaii. Hiking the beautiful mountains of Hawaii has become one of my habits since we moved to Honolulu in 2004. I must have made two dozen or so hiking trips so far and I plan to write about them before my memories fade away. Growing up in Çanakkale, Turkey, with panoramic views of Dardanelle, I have always enjoyed observing the nature. My attraction to the mountains however has developed in Hawaii. The picturesque views of Hawaii’s mountains strike me as surreal. As I walk from UH Manoa Campus to our apartment in the UH Faculty Housing in Manoa almost every evening during the week, I can’t take my eyes off of the twin peaks of Konahuanui on the left and Mount Olympus on the right and the ridge between them. Mountains in general evoke feelings of majesty and awe in me, and I perceive them as the work of a great artist. I also associate the mountains with the idea of freedom. Dadaloğlu, a 19th century poet of southern Turkey, rebels against the Sultan by saying

 

Ferman Padişahın, Dağlar Bizimdir

 

translated as

 

Mandate is the Sultan’s, Mountains are Ours.

 

Listening to the wind while standing on a razor-edge ridge with steep drops on both sides is something I have not quite done, but I already know that such an experience would completely fill me with the feeling of freedom. So, it looks like I will continue to hike.

 

OK, but why do I want to write? This is strange for I have never had a desire to write in the past. "What am I going to write about?" was my typical response to the writing assignments in the middle school. My 9 year old son Esad now shows a similar reaction to his writing assignments. Surprisingly, however, my 15 year old daughter Esma wants to be a writer; here is an essay by Esma on our Koko Head Crater adventure. As far as my own desire to write now, I am not sure where it is coming from.  Maybe, I would like to leave something personal to my children about their father. Maybe, reading Dayle Turner’s Oahu Hike Tales and Kaleo Lancaster’s Island Trails has something to do with my desire to write about my own hiking experience. Maybe, I’d like to write because it helps me to crystallize my thoughts. Maybe, I have too much time in my hands. Whatever the reasons may be, I intend to add some lines to this blog every time I do a trail.

 

 

Sunday, March 14, 2010

 

Exactly a week ago, on Sunday, I and Hamed tackled one of my latest unsolved problems, namely the twin peaks of Konahuanui known as K1 and K2. We started at the Kalawahine trailhead at 7:40 am, and shortly turned left on the Pauoa Flats trail. At 8:20 am, we were at the Nuuanu Lookout area where there is a bench in honor of a fallen hiker. We took a look at the water reservoir, and turned right to take the trail leading to the twin peaks. In about half an hour, we saw a yellow birdfeeder hung on a branch. Here, we took our first set of pictures and continued upward. It was drizzling pretty much throughout but on this part it also rained on us for several minutes. The twin peaks and the ridge leading to them were veiled in fog. We were hoping that the fog would clear up by the time we got to the top. There were some steep and slippery uphill sections in which I had to employ the ``put-one-foot-in-front-of-the-other’’ approach. We walked on a narrow ridge with sharp drops on both sides. Looking down, we could not see the bottom due to the fog, which may have helped us keep our sanity. It was windy, and I enjoyed the sound of the wind. Later, Hamed estimated the wind speed as about 25 mph. At 10:00 am, we reached a high-point which turned out to be K1, but at the time we were not exactly sure where we were because of poor visibility. We descended ahead in hopes of reaching K2, or first K1 and then K2. This section was overgrown and very muddy. We thought that we were in the dip between K1 and K2, but could not see anything high in front of us for a while. Then, all of a sudden, we came across a magnificent view of an uphill slope rising steeply into the fog. Hamed used the word ``intimidating’’ to describe this view. We pressed on. During our final steps, we heard some birds singing. I saw one of them flying. It was a pretty small thing. Hamed thought that they were love birds.  We reached K2 at 10:45 am. 

 

We chatted while having some fruits and snacks atop K2. We took some pictures of each other with no views of course. Then, we started heading back down at 11:20am. For few minutes, the birds accompanied us again with their song. Shortly after, I also heard an airplane flying by but I could not see it. We did not talk much on the way back as we had to concentrate a lot to avoid any slip and fall. Descending through the muddy slopes required caution and care. Often, there were footholds and ropes to help getting down. However, I remember one section where I intend to fix rope if I do this trail again. Hamed later said that he did longer trails before but this was the one that required the most concentration. When we got back to the Nuuanu Lookout area, we saw two ladies sitting on the bench. We exchanged greetings with them. Up to this point, we had not seen anyone on the trail. We continued back on the Pauoa Flats trail on which we came across few other hikers. To our dismay, the part between the bench and the trailhead took appreciably longer on the return trip. I remember speeding up just to finish it. We reached the trailhead at 2:20pm, six and a half hour after we started. 

 

Overall, this was one of my most memorable hikes. We had no views and the mud bath between K1 and K2 was so ridiculous that after while I did not even care whether or not I was stepping right into the mud. However, in a way, we did this hike in an ideal weather condition. The cool weather, the wind, and the fog created a natural air conditioning effect, and at the end I was not nearly as tired as I could have otherwise been. The wind and the poor visibility also made me feel like I was climbing the real K2! There was a point on the way back where Hamed was perhaps 20 ft. in front of me and because of the wind noise I had to yell to be heard. It was a good workout and we managed to get to the top. I enjoyed Hamed’s company. I thought that I slowed him a bit and appreciated his patience, but he disagreed with this assessment and he said that we kept a good pace. I certainly hope to do many other trails with him as well as this trail again on a clear day. When Hamed drove me back home in the afternoon, the fog cleared up to open the beautiful view of the twin peaks from Manoa. In front of our unit, my wife took a picture of me hosing down myself to get the mud off of my pants and shoes. Later in the evening, we went to one of our friend’s house for dinner. I was surprised how long I lasted on that day.
